<a href="http://2.bp.blogspot.com/_ADwvfqkxChw/RhNv3OUAeXI/AAAAAAAAAPE/3WcRNsqURyc/s1600-h/aec.jpg"><img id="BLOGGER_PHOTO_ID_5049502601737959794" style="margin: 0pt 10px 10px 0pt; float: left; cursor: pointer;" alt="" src="/assets/uploads/external/2.bp.blogspot.com/_ADwvfqkxChw/RhNv3OUAeXI/AAAAAAAAAPE/3WcRNsqURyc/s320/aec.jpg" border="0" /></a>There is only one London, New York, Tokyo etc yet there are many digital models of each city. London has, at last count, 6 models of varying accuracy and detail, the majority of which are created and held by architecture practices. Investment in the creation of such models can run into six figures and as such the use of highly detailed models are based on commercial licences and restrictive <span>IP</span>. At Digital Urban we hold the view of an open source model allowing the public free access to data and visualisation. It is after all our city and we should have the ability to visualise changes in the public domain. There are of course a number of issues with this point of view, see the recent <a href="http://technology.guardian.co.uk/weekly/story/0,,1981821,00.html">Guardian Article </a>for more info.<br /><br />As such we think that their should be a single definitive model of London that architects contribute too and use. Data quality issues would be put in place, compared with say the Google 3D Warehouse, and the model would be held in both Professional <span>GIS</span>, CAD and public access <span>KML</span> formats. Gareth Munro, Managing Director of <a href="http://www.designhive.co.uk/"><span>Designhive</span></a> refreshingly holds the same view, one which is covered in an article in the March/April edition of <span>AEC</span> Magazine. In an extract from the article Gareth states that:<br /><br />A<span style="font-style: italic;"><span>lmost</span> certainly, whoever has the most detailed and accurate model of London has the competitive edge. The ability to create a 3D model of a building or other development and simply ‘drop it’ into an accurate model of London quickly and efficiently will inevitably win the support, backing and perhaps more importantly the business of architects and developers in</span><span style="font-style: italic;"> the future.<br /><br /></span>He continues,<span style="font-style: italic;"><br /></span><p style="font-style: italic;">However, the creation of multiple London models may provide competitive advantage to some and an instant win for those that ‘have’, but in the long-term does little to save time and cost for architects, developers and the wider industry.</p><p style="font-style: italic;"><a href="http://1.bp.blogspot.com/_ADwvfqkxChw/RhNza-UAeYI/AAAAAAAAAPM/mGOohXgVyCY/s1600-h/aec2.jpg"><img id="BLOGGER_PHOTO_ID_5049506514453166466" style="margin: 0px auto 10px; display: block; cursor: pointer; text-align: center;" alt="" src="/assets/uploads/external/1.bp.blogspot.com/_ADwvfqkxChw/RhNza-UAeYI/AAAAAAAAAPM/mGOohXgVyCY/s320/aec2.jpg" border="0" /></a></p>The article is focused on the Virtual London model held at <a href="http://www.casa.ucl.ac.uk/">our lab</a> (see our <a href="http://digitalurban.blogspot.com/search/label/Virtual%20London">Virtual London thread</a> for more details ) and interestingly the whole magazine can be viewed online, free of charge and without registration in <span>PDF</span> format.<br /><br /><a href="http://aecmag.com/index.php?option=com_content&task=view&amp;amp;amp;amp;amp;amp;amp;id=161&Itemid=36">Read the article online</a> or <a href="http://www.aecmag.com/pdf/">view the magazine in <span>PDF</span></a>.<br /><br />So should there be one model? How do other cities deal with digital models and getting architects to contribute with a view of online <span>distribution</span>?

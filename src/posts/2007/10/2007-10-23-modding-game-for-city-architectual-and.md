<p><a href="http://3.bp.blogspot.com/_ADwvfqkxChw/Rx3ERYE2mZI/AAAAAAAAAvo/KTQuvhlBYcU/s1600-h/water_61293.jpg"><img style="margin: 0pt 10px 10px 0pt; float: left; cursor: pointer;" src="/assets/uploads/external/3.bp.blogspot.com/_ADwvfqkxChw/Rx3ERYE2mZI/AAAAAAAAAvo/KTQuvhlBYcU/s200/water_61293.jpg" alt="" id="BLOGGER_PHOTO_ID_5124467753817119122" border="0" /></a>Modifying games for visualisation is intriguing, it opens up the game engine for real time data manipulation and with it access to some of the most cutting edge <span>shaders</span> and lighting/weather effects currently available. Yet, as we have discussed before see <a href="http://digitalurban.blogspot.com/search/label/Architecture">Game Engines for Architects? Architecture for Gamers? Escapist Article</a>, mention gaming to academics/architects/city planners and you come up against a lot of opposition and scepticism.<br /><br />With the new <a href="http://www.crysisdemo.com/"><span>Crysis</span> demo</a> just around the corner it is looking like the <span>Crysis</span> Engine could give Oblivion a run for its money in terms of architectural visualisation but many other games can also be modified.<br /><br />We are not recommending that commercial companies sit their clients down with a modded version of the Grand Theft Auto series of games but a lot of genuinely innovative work has been carried out with such engines.<br /><br />One of which is the Back to the Future Mod which has reconstructed scenes from the film and subsequently imported them into the game - the image below is the main town square in <span>pre</span>-game form:<br /><br /><a

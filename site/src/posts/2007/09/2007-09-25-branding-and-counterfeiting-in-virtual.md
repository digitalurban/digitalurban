<p><a href="http://2.bp.blogspot.com/_ADwvfqkxChw/RvkbuIE2lyI/AAAAAAAAAqw/U5C-lHD5XP4/s1600-h/secondlife-nike.jpg"><img style="margin: 0pt 0pt 10px 10px; float: right; cursor: pointer;" src="/assets/uploads/external/2.bp.blogspot.com/_ADwvfqkxChw/RvkbuIE2lyI/AAAAAAAAAqw/U5C-lHD5XP4/s320/secondlife-nike.jpg" alt="" id="BLOGGER_PHOTO_ID_5114149331111679778" border="0" /></a><a href="http://www.secondlife.com/">Second Life</a> and its predecessors such as <a href="http://www.activeworlds.com/"><span>ActiveWorlds</span></a> represent the best in web 2.0 - the ability to reconstruct any real world object in the virtual environment. With this however comes the murky waters of copyright of real world objects in a digital world where objects are viewed as simply data.<br /><br />We started thinking about such issues when we noticed the Nike logo on our trainers which are part our persona in Second Life. The trainers were given to us by a fellow Second Life citizen without a thought to copyright or payment for the use of the design.<br /><br />Yet in Virtual Worlds products do not have to be manufactured, they are simply made once and then copied as many times as needs be, often by users simply swapping goods between themselves.<br /><br />Within Second Life there is a thriving economy and many real world brands do have a legitimate presence in the environment. The movie embedded below by <span style="display: inline;"><a href="http://www.personalizemedia.com/">Gary Hayes at <span>PersonlizeMedia</span>.com</a> provides an overview of brands in Second Life:<br /><br /><center><object height="350" width="425"><param name="movie" value="http://www.youtube.com/v/tEGHJuCbGdo"><param name="wmode" value="transparent"><embed src="http://www.youtube.com/v/tEGHJuCbGdo" type="application/x-shockwave-flash" wmode="transparent" height="350" width="425"></embed></object></center><br /><br />Law Firm <span>Evensheds</span> have issued a warning that legal action maybe around the corner for users selling fake goods in virtual worlds, the <a href="http://www.theretailbulletin.com/?tag=ea4c796cccfc3899b5f9ae2874237c20">Retail Bulletin has a good article</a> on this exploring the problems of legal actions on users that are in all essence virtual.<br /><br />The same issues relate to data of the traditionally geographic kind, we have been able to import building outlines into Second Life but does this infringe copyright of the Ordnance Survey? Without doubt the answer is yes and in light of the current view of  the Ordnance Survey on licensing, importing into Second Life is an issue that is not worth pursuing further (see <a href="http://digitalurban.blogspot.com/2007/08/google-disappointed-ordnance-survey.html">Google and the Ordnance Survey: Disappointed</a>).</span><br /><span style="display: inline;"><br />Along similar lines does reconstructing the built environment in virtual worlds infringe copyright on use of branding with say importing a Woolworth's retail unit to communicate built form? Issues of copyright of geographic data are however perhaps minor in terms of the blatant cloning of real world goods such as trainers, cars and clothing in Second Life.<br /><br />The looming threat of legal action is however worrying, if we can copy real world objects and use them virtually surely it is flattering to the company and not an issue of future court proceedings?<br /><br />Then again....<br /></span></p>
